Part 01 — Orientation
The IOI System: What Actually Happens

You have spent your competitive programming life on Codeforces and AtCoder, and that is excellent training — but those websites quietly do a job for you that nobody will do at the IOI. When you press Submit on Codeforces, their servers compile your code, feed it hidden input files, capture your output, compare it against expected answers, and print a verdict. At the IOI, you sit in front of a Linux computer, and for five hours that machine is your entire world: you compile your own code in a terminal, you feed it test files yourself, you compare outputs yourself, and only the final submission goes to the contest server. Nothing here is difficult — but every step must be automatic, because in a five-hour contest every confused minute is a lost subtask.

This manual builds that automation into your hands, keystroke by keystroke. It is written for exactly one reader: a strong competitive programmer on a MacBook Pro who has never seriously touched a terminal. Everything in it is taken from the real, current IOI system — the official contest rules, the real 2025 and 2026 contest environments, and the actual task files that contestants downloaded at IOI 2025 in Bolivia. Nothing is simplified, shortened, or faked.

1.1The format of the game

The International Olympiad in Informatics runs two contest days. On each day you get three tasks and five hours to work on them. Each task is worth 100 points, so a full IOI is scored out of 600. There is no rating, no leaderboard during the contest, and no hacks — just you, three problems, and a clock. Medals are awarded by final score: roughly the top tenth of contestants receive gold, the next fifth silver, and the next third bronze. The exact cutoffs are set after each contest, but the practical message never changes: partial credit is everything. A contestant who reliably extracts 40 points from problems they cannot fully solve is medaling; a contestant who only submits complete solutions is not.

Before the first contest day there is also a two-hour practice competition with the same software, the same submissions, and the same grading system. Its tasks are published in advance, and you are even allowed to bring printed solutions on paper for it. Treat that session as sacred: it is your one guaranteed chance to test the entire pipeline — login, download, compile, submit, feedback — with zero stakes. This manual ends with you being fully ready to squeeze every drop of value out of those two hours.

1.2Subtasks: how 100 points are actually split

Every IOI task is divided into subtasks — groups of hidden test cases, each group with its own point value and its own (easier) constraints. A subtask is scored all-or-nothing within itself: your solution must pass every test case in the group to earn its points. This is the single most important structural fact about the IOI, so let's make it concrete with the real task Festival from IOI 2025. Its official data contains 115 test cases grouped into 8 subtasks: the sample tests form subtask 0 worth 0 points, and the remaining seven scored subtasks carry the 100 points, ordered from tiny special cases (small N, simple structures) up to the full-constraint final group.

The consequences are beautiful for a well-prepared contestant. First, the samples you can see in the statement are worth nothing by themselves — they exist to let you verify your understanding. Second, a brute-force program that only handles tiny inputs still earns real points in the early subtasks. Third, the subtask table in each statement is literally a menu of intermediate goals: when you are stuck at 100 points, the path to 60 is written down in front of you in the constraints column. You will learn to read that table the way a chess player reads a board.

1.3The scoring formula, precisely

The official rules define the scoring in three layers, and each layer works in your favor once you understand it:

  • Per test case: your program runs once per test. If it exceeds the time or memory limit, or crashes, or prints a wrong answer, that test scores 0.
  • Per subtask: the subtask's score is the minimum across its test cases — one failing test poisons the whole group.
  • Per task: your final score for each subtask is the maximum that subtask ever achieved across all your submissions, and the task score is their sum.

That last rule deserves a bold box in your head: scores never go down when you resubmit. The official rules even give a worked example: submit once and score 30 on subtask 1 and 10 on subtask 2; submit again and score 0 on subtask 1 but 40 on subtask 2 — your final score is 70, the best of each. This is why the single most reliable IOI strategy is to submit early, submit partial solutions, and keep improving. A brute force submitted in the first hour banks points that no later bug can take away from you.

1.4What you may and may not do

The submission rules are short and worth memorizing, because violating them costs real contest time. You may submit at most once per minute per task (this rate limit is lifted in the last 15 minutes). You may make at most 50 submissions per task across the whole contest — far more than you should ever need, but not infinite, so never submit blind. Your source file must be a single C++ file of at most 50 KiB. The grading server must be able to compile it in 10 seconds and 512 MiB, which no honest program ever approaches. Multi-threading is allowed, but with a catch: the measured running time is the sum of all threads' time, so threads buy you nothing unless the task explicitly says otherwise. And since 2025, the defining rule: your submission must not read standard input, write standard output, or touch any file — all data flows through the task's official interface, which is exactly why Part 7 of this manual exists.

Your Mac's default compiler is Apple's (a rebranded Clang) while the IOI uses real GCC. For learning, they are identical: same flags, same C++20, same behavior for anything you will write. Two tiny differences (a missing <bits/stdc++.h> header and occasional warning text) are handled in Part 3, and the optional real-GCC install removes them completely.

Part 03 — Setup
Turn Your Mac Into the Contest Machine

This part installs your toolkit. Do it once, in order, and by the end you will have a terminal, a C++ compiler matching the IOI's, and a folder system identical to what you would build on the contest machine. Total time: about 20 minutes, most of it waiting for downloads. Every command here is complete — copy it exactly.

3.1Open Terminal, your new best friend
1
Find and open the Terminal app
Press Cmd+Space to open Spotlight, type Terminal, and press Enter. A white (or black) window appears with a line ending in %. That window is the Terminal, and the program running inside it is called a shell (on your Mac it is zsh; the IOI machines run bash — same language for everything you will do). Pin Terminal to your Dock: right-click its Dock icon → Options → Keep in Dock.
2
Read the prompt
The line looks like firstname@MacBook-Pro ~ %. The part before the % tells you who and where you are; everything you type goes after it. The ~ means your home folder. You never need to type the prompt itself — the shell prints it for you.
3.2Install the compiler (Apple's, 2 minutes)

Macs ship without a compiler, but Apple provides one free through the Command Line Tools. In Terminal, type this line and press Enter:

Terminal — install Command Line Tools
$ xcode-select --install
xcode-select: note: install requested for command line developer tools

A system window pops up asking to install the tools. Click Install, accept the license when asked, and wait for the progress bar (typically 2–5 minutes; it downloads around 1.5 GB). When a small dialog says the installation completed, verify it worked:

Terminal — verify the compiler exists
$ g++ --version
Apple clang version 17.0.0 ...
Target: arm64-apple-darwin25.0.0
InstalledDir: /Library/Developer/CommandLineTools/usr/bin

Any version number means success. Note carefully what you are looking at: on a Mac, the command g++ is actually Apple Clang wearing GCC's name — and that is fine. It fully supports C++20 and every flag in this manual.

3.3Install real GCC (the contest's compiler, 10 minutes)

For a 100% contest-matching compiler — including the bits/stdc++.h header that GCC has and Clang lacks — install Homebrew (the standard Mac package manager) and then GCC through it. This step is optional but recommended, and Part 5's template is written to work either way.

Terminal — Homebrew, then GCC
$ /bin/bash -c "$(curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Homebrew/install/HEAD/install.sh)"
(enter your Mac password when asked; wait for the installer to finish)

# The installer prints two lines under "Next steps" — run them, they add brew to your PATH:
$ echo 'eval "$(/opt/homebrew/bin/brew shellenv)"' >> ~/.zprofile
$ eval "$(/opt/homebrew/bin/brew shellenv)"

$ brew install gcc
(several minutes of downloading and installing)

$ g++-15 --version
g++-15 (Homebrew GCC ...) 15.x.x
Two compilers, two names — keep this straight

After this, g++ is still Apple Clang and g++-15 (or g++-14, whatever version brew printed) is real GCC. Throughout this manual, commands use g++ for simplicity — substitute g++-15 if you want the contest compiler. The one visible difference you might hit: #include <bits/stdc++.h> compiles with GCC but fails with Clang. The template in Part 5 avoids it entirely, so both compilers work everywhere in this manual.

3.4Wire up VS Code
1
Open your practice folder in VS Code — not loose files
At the IOI you work inside one task folder at a time; copy that habit now. In VS Code: File → Open Folder... → create and select a folder named ioi in your home directory. The sidebar now shows that folder as your workspace, and new files land inside it automatically.
2
Learn the one shortcut that changes everything
Press Ctrl+` (Control + backtick, the key under Esc). VS Code opens its integrated terminal in a panel at the bottom — same shell, same commands, but now it lives next to your code. Press the same shortcut to hide it. This single shortcut is how you will work for the rest of your life: code on top, terminal below.
3
Optional: the C/C++ extension
Click the Extensions icon in the left sidebar (four squares), search C/C++ (by Microsoft), and click Install. It adds IntelliSense autocomplete and red squiggles for errors while you type. The IOI machines have it preinstalled too. You do not need to configure anything — the terminal remains where compiling actually happens.
3.5Create the folder system

Organization is free speed. In the VS Code terminal (or Terminal.app — same thing), run these lines one by one. They build the exact structure you will recreate for every task at the IOI, and open . pops it open in Finder so you can see what you built:

Part 04 — Fundamentals
Terminal From Absolute Zero

A terminal is a text window where you talk to the computer by typing commands instead of clicking icons. That is the entire mystery. Each command is a small program with a name — ls, cd, g++ — and learning the terminal is just learning a vocabulary of about fifteen words. You type a command, press Enter, the computer answers, and a fresh prompt appears. Nothing you type in this part can break anything: worst case, an error message appears and you try again. Work through this part with a real terminal open, typing every line yourself — reading without typing builds nothing.

4.1Where am I? — pwd and ls

The two most-used commands in existence answer the only two questions a shell ever asks: where am I? and what is here?

pwd — print working directory
$ pwd
/Users/yourname
# "I am in my home folder." pwd never changes anything — it only reports.
ls — list what is here
$ ls
Desktop  Documents  Downloads  ioi  Movies  Pictures
$ ls -a        # -a = also show hidden files (names starting with a dot)
.  ..  .zshrc  Desktop  Documents  ioi ...
$ ls -l        # -l = one per line, with sizes and dates
drwxr-xr-x   5 yourname  staff   160 Aug 29 10:12 ioi

Those extra letters like -a are called flags: they modify a command's behavior, always written with a dash. You will use the same idea with the compiler later — g++ -O2 is exactly this pattern. Flags can be combined (ls -la), and this flag grammar is identical on your Mac and on the IOI's Linux machines.

4.2Moving around — cd

You move between folders with cd (change directory). Four shortcuts cover ninety percent of all movement: a folder's name to enter it, .. to go up one level, ~ to jump home, and cd alone to jump home as well. Watch a full trip:

cd — the only movement command you need
$ cd ioi              # enter the ioi folder
$ pwd
/Users/yourname/ioi
$ cd 2025/festival   # enter nested folders in one step
$ pwd
/Users/yourname/ioi/2025/festival
$ cd ..               # up one level
$ cd                  # bare cd = straight home
$ pwd
/Users/yourname

Two habits will save you from the classic beginner frustration. First, Tab completion: type cd fes and press Tab — the shell completes it to festival automatically. If two folders start with the same letters, pressing Tab twice shows the options. Tab completes commands, folder names, and file names; professionals use it constantly, which is why they never typo paths. Second, drag and drop: dragging a folder from Finder onto the terminal window types its full path for you — handy for awkward locations.

4.3Making things — mkdir and touch
mkdir and touch — create folders and empty files
$ cd ~/ioi/2025/festival
$ mkdir tests          # make a folder named tests
$ mkdir -p a/b/c      # -p = create the whole chain at once
$ touch sol.cpp        # create an empty file (or update its date)
$ ls
a  sol.cpp  tests

In practice you will create code files from VS Code (File → New File → name it sol.cpp), and touch mainly earns its keep when a script or a quick example needs an empty file fast. Both ways end in the same place: a file on disk that the terminal can see.

4.4Reading files — cat, head, tail
cat — print a file's whole content
$ cat sol.cpp          # print the file to the screen
#include <iostream>
int main() { std::cout << "hi"; }
$ head -3 sol.cpp      # first 3 lines only
$ tail -3 sol.cpp      # last 3 lines only

You will use cat constantly with test files, because it is the fastest way to ask "what exactly is inside 01.in?" — a question you will ask hundreds of times in your IOI life. For long outputs, head and tail show just the ends, which is usually all you need.

4.5The keys that feel like superpowers

It is not frozen; it is busy forever. Ctrl+C kills it instantly and hands you back the prompt. Nothing is broken, nothing is lost; just fix the loop and run again. At the IOI, this keystroke has saved more contest hours than any algorithm trick.

Type this sequence yourself (not copy-paste — typing is the point) and predict each output before pressing Enter. It rehearses the exact skeleton you will build under time pressure at the contest:

The 20% that gives 80%

Part 05 — The Build Loop
Compile and Run C++ the IOI Way

Compiling means translating your human-readable sol.cpp into a machine-executable file. On Codeforces this happens invisibly on their servers; at the IOI it is a command you type. In this part you will compile your first program with the exact flags the IOI grading system uses, learn what each flag means, run programs with keyboard input, and set up debug and timing builds — the complete build loop of a contest day.

5.1Your first compile

In VS Code, inside your ~/ioi/2025/festival folder, create a file named sum.cpp with this content — a program that reads two integers and prints their sum:

sum.cpp
#include <iostream>
using namespace std;

int main() {
    long long a, b;
    cin >> a >> b;
    cout << a + b << "\n";
    return 0;
}

Now compile it with the official IOI recipe. This is the real compile command from the IOI 2025 contest files, with one adjustment noted below:

Terminal — compile with contest flags
$ cd ~/ioi/2025/festival
$ g++ -std=gnu++20 -Wall -O2 -pipe -g -o sum sum.cpp
$ ls
examples  sum  sum.cpp

If the shell answers with silence and a new prompt — that is success. The compiler only speaks when something is wrong. Notice the new green (executable) file sum sitting next to sum.cpp: that binary is your program now. Here is what every piece of that command does, because at 3 hours into a contest you should be able to reconstruct it from understanding, not memory:

PieceMeaning
g++The compiler itself (use g++-15 for the contest-matching GCC)
-std=gnu++20Use the C++20 language standard — the one the IOI graders use
-WallWarn about all suspicious code (unused variables, comparing signed with unsigned...). Always on, always read them
-O2Optimize for speed — this is what the grader measures, so test with it
-pipeMinor speedup of the compiler itself (cosmetic, but part of the official line)
-gKeep debug symbols in the binary, so crash messages and debuggers are useful
-o sumName the output executable sum (otherwise it defaults to a.out)
sum.cppThe source file to compile
About -static, the one flag we dropped

The official IOI script also passes -static, which bakes the C++ library into the binary so it runs on any machine. It is a server-side convenience that macOS's compiler does not fully support, so leave it off at home — it changes nothing about how your program behaves. On a Linux machine (or the VM from Part 13) the full official line g++ -std=gnu++20 -Wall -O2 -pipe -static -g works as-is.

5.2Run it — and feed it input by hand
Terminal — run and type input
$ ./sum
3 4
7
$

Three things happened there. The ./ prefix means "the file named sum in this folder" — the shell will not run programs from the current folder unless you spell that out, a safety rule shared by macOS and Linux. Then the program waited silently for input: you typed 3 4, pressed Enter, and it printed 7. When the program waits for more input than you want to give, press Ctrl+D to signal "end of input" — remember that key, it ends the wait and lets the program finish.

5.3When compilation fails — read only the first error
A typical error session
$ g++ -std=gnu++20 -Wall -O2 -g -o sum sum.cpp
sum.cpp:5:12: error: expected ';' after expression
    cout << a + b << "\n"
           ^
           ;
$

Compiler errors look terrifying and are actually polite: they name the file:line:column, say what they expected, and often draw an arrow at the exact character. The iron rule is fix only the first error, then recompile — later errors are usually echoes of the first one and vanish with it. Also learn the difference in tone: error means no binary was produced, while warning means it compiled but the compiler suspects a bug. Treat warnings as free code review; contestants who read them find their bugs an hour earlier than contestants who do not.

5.4The debug build — your bug-hunting compiler

There is a second compile mode that trades speed for x-ray vision. The -fsanitize=address,undefined flags make the program check itself while running: out-of-bounds array access, use of uninitialized memory, integer overflow — the exact bugs that silently pass samples and detonate on hidden tests. The sanitizer does not catch wrong algorithms, but it catches illegal memory behavior, which is roughly half of all contest crashes.

Terminal — debug build
$ g++ -std=gnu++20 -Wall -g -fsanitize=address,undefined -o sumdbg sum.cpp
$ ./sumdbg
3 4
7

Keep the two builds side by side during practice: sum is the fast build you will submit, sumdbg is the truth-teller you run when something misbehaves. When sumdbg crashes, it prints a report naming the exact line of the bad access — read the first paragraph of that report and you are usually done. The IOI machines support sanitizers too, and they cost nothing to have in your toolbox.

5.5Printing to yourself — cerr

The other debugging primitive is printing from inside the program. The trick is to print to standard error instead of standard output, with cerr. Standard output is the sacred channel that gets compared against expected answers — pollute it and every correct program becomes wrong. Standard error is your private channel: it appears on screen, never reaches the answer comparison, and (this is official IOI policy) writing to it is explicitly allowed in submissions.

cerr — debug prints that never count
int main() {
    long long a, b;
    cin >> a >> b;
    cerr << "debug: read a=" << a << " b=" << b << "\n";   // private channel
    cout << a + b << "\n";                              // the real answer
}

Build a reflex: the moment a program behaves strangely, drop one cerr at each suspicious step — what did I actually read? what is the loop counter? did I even reach this branch? — and watch the story unfold. Before submitting, sweep the cerr lines away (or keep them; they are legal). Many medalists leave them in permanently because they cost nothing on the grading server.

5.6How fast is it? — time
time — measure a run
$ time ./sum < big.in
1987654
real    0m0.84s
user    0m0.83s
sys     0m0.01s

The time command runs another command and reports how long it took: real is wall-clock time, which is the number that matters. Tasks at the IOI publish their time limit (often 1–3 seconds), and the rules promise limits are generous — roughly double what the intended solution needs. Your private rule of thumb during practice: if your solution takes more than half the stated limit on your laptop, it is too slow for the grading server, because server hardware differs from your Mac. Half the limit is green; the limit itself is a red alert.

5.7The contest template

Here is a starting template built for the IOI: no bits/stdc++.h (so it compiles on both your Mac's Clang and the contest's GCC), fast input setup, and a debug macro that disappears unless you ask for it. Save it once as template.cpp inside ~/ioi and copy it into every new task folder. Note: you may not carry prewritten code into the IOI contest hall, but a template like this is something you will retype from memory in ninety seconds, and practicing with it builds exactly that speed.

template.cpp — works on Mac and on the contest machine
#include <bits/stdc++.h>   // if you installed Homebrew GCC, you may use this single line
                          // and delete the eight includes below — both versions work
#include <algorithm>
#include <cmath>
#include <cstdio>
#include <iostream>
#include <map>
#include <queue>
#include <set>
#include <vector>

using namespace std;

int main() {
    ios_base::sync_with_stdio(false);   // the two lines that make cin/cout fast
    cin.tie(nullptr);

    long long n;
    cin >> n;

    cout << n * 2 << "\n";
    return 0;
}
long long, always long long

Notice the template reads into long long by default. IOI constraints routinely let products and sums exceed 32-bit range, and "works on samples, overflows on test 40" is the most heart-breaking failure in the sport. When in doubt — and even when not in doubt — use long long for anything that can grow.

Part 06 — The Core Skill
Test Cases: the .in / .out System

This is the chapter you asked for when you said "I have seen the input output zip but I don't know how to use it." Here is the entire secret in one sentence: a test case is just two text files — an .in file holding the input, and an .out (sometimes .ans) file holding the expected output. The judge runs your program with the .in file poured into its standard input, captures what your program prints, and compares that against the .out file. Every testing tool in the world — from the IOI graders to your own scripts — is a variation of that loop. Master the loop by hand once, and every system you ever meet becomes obvious.

First, orient yourself with the flow diagram. Every piece of it is a command you already know or are about to learn:

Input file 01.in — the data the judge will feed your program
Your program ./sum < 01.in — reads it, computes, prints to standard output
Your output ./sum < 01.in > my.out — captured into a file
Comparison diff my.out 01.out — identical means PASS, any difference means FAIL
6.1Make a test case with your own hands

Stay in ~/ioi/2025/festival with the compiled sum from Part 5. A test needs input and expected output, so create both. The fastest way to make a small file is echo, which prints whatever you give it — and with > it prints into a file:

Terminal — create the input file
$ cd ~/ioi/2025/festival
$ mkdir tests
$ echo "3 4" > tests/01.in
$ cat tests/01.in
3 4

(For longer tests, create the file in VS Code instead — right-click the tests folder in the sidebar → New File → type the input → save. The file is the same file no matter which tool made it.) Now the moment of truth — feed the file to your program with input redirection, the < symbol:

Terminal — feed input from a file
$ ./sum < tests/01.in
7

That output appeared on your screen, but for comparison you need it in a file. Output redirection, the > symbol, captures it. Then write the expected answer by hand and compare — this exact three-line ritual is the heartbeat of all competitive programming outside a browser:

Terminal — capture, expect, compare
$ ./sum < tests/01.in > tests/01.my   # run, capture my output
$ echo "7" > tests/01.out             # the expected answer (by hand)
$ diff tests/01.my tests/01.out          # compare mine vs expected
$                                             # silence = identical = PASS
diff's silence confuses everyone once

diff prints nothing at all when the two files are identical. Beginners stare at the empty screen waiting for a "correct!" that never comes. Silence is the compliment: no differences found. When files differ, diff prints the differing lines with < marking yours and > marking the expected — a compact, readable report of exactly how you are wrong.

6.2The PASS / FAIL one-liner you will type forever

Shells have a wonderful feature: && chains two commands so the second runs only if the first succeeded, and || runs its command only if the first failed. Since diff succeeds exactly when files match, this composes into a self-announcing verdict:

The verdict one-liner, both outcomes
$ ./sum < tests/01.in | diff - tests/01.out && echo PASS || echo FAIL
PASS

$ ./sum < tests/01.in | diff - tests/01.out && echo PASS || echo FAIL
1c1
< 8
---
> 7
FAIL

Two new characters appeared there. The pipe | sends the left command's output directly into the right command's input — so diff - compares "whatever arrives through the pipe" (the dash means read from the pipe instead of a file) against the expected file. No temporary .my file needed. This one line is the entire manual testing workflow, compressed: run, pipe, diff, verdict.

6.3Test them all — the loop

Real tasks have dozens of tests, so typing the one-liner per file gets old in about a minute. Shells solve this with the for loop, which runs a block once per matching file. This is the honest grandparent of every auto-judge script, and later in Part 8 you will grow it into a full grader:

Run every test in the folder
$ for f in tests/*.in; do ./sum < "$f" | diff - "${f%.in}.out" > /dev/null && echo "PASS $f" || echo "FAIL $f"; done
PASS tests/01.in
PASS tests/02.in
FAIL tests/03.in

Read it slowly, because this line is dense but never mystical: for f in tests/*.in walks every file ending in .in (the * wildcard matches any name, and the shell expands it before the loop even starts); "$f" is the current file; and ${f%.in}.out is shell-speak for "the same name but ending in .out" — it cuts the .in suffix and glues on .out, turning 01.in into 01.out. The > /dev/null throws away diff's chatter so only your PASS/FAIL verdicts remain.

0

6.6Pitfalls — the four classic false alarms
  • Trailing whitespace and line breaks. diff compares byte by byte, so a stray space at a line's end reads as a difference even when the answer looks right. If diff complains about a line that looks identical, suspect invisible characters — cat -A tests/01.out (on Linux) or turning on "render whitespace" in VS Code exposes them. The IOI graders are more forgiving than raw diff about trailing whitespace, but build the habit of clean output anyway: print numbers separated by single spaces and end lines with "\n".
  • Missing final newline. If your file lacks the last line break, diff prints a cryptic \ No newline at end of file. Not fatal, but normalize it away by ending every output with a newline.
  • Wrong extension pairing. Your script hunts .out but the download used .ans — everything "fails" for no visible reason. The judge script in Part 8 checks both extensions automatically.
  • Multiple valid answers. Some tasks accept several correct outputs ("print any valid schedule"), where your answer and the expected file differ while both are right. Raw diff says FAIL; the real grader uses a special checker. When a statement says "any", believe it — and rely on the sample grader or the online judge instead of plain diff.
Part 07 — The Real Thing
The Modern IOI Task: Interface + Sample Grader

Now the most important modernization to understand — because if you practiced only with old IOI archives, contest day would surprise you. Since IOI 2025, tasks no longer read cin and print cout. Instead, each task gives you an interface: a set of functions you must implement, and a grader program that calls your functions for you. Your submission is still a single C++ file, but its shape changed.

Look at two genuine signatures from IOI 2025. In Festival, you implement one function and simply return the answer:

festival.h — the real IOI 2025 interface
#include <vector>

std::vector<int> max_coupons(int A, std::vector<int> P, std::vector<int> T);

In Souvenirs, the flow runs both ways: you implement buy_souvenirs, and inside it you may call a grader function named transaction(...) to interact with the (hidden) shop — up to 5000 calls per test. That style is called a communication or interactive task. A third style, output-only, asks you to submit answer files computed by any means you like, no program at all. One IOI now mixes all three styles across its six tasks, which is exactly why the downloadable task package matters so much.

7.1The zip you download from CMS

During the contest, each task's page on CMS offers a zip package. You saw people use these folders — here is the complete anatomy of a real one (Festival, IOI 2025), so "some other stuff" becomes five files you know by name:

7.4The run script — and why 2 GB of stack matters

The official run script is three lines, and the middle two are quietly important:

The official run script (from IOI 2025 data)
memory=2097152    # 2 GB total memory cap — the contest limit
stack_size=2097152 # 2 GB STACK cap — deep recursion is legal here!
ulimit -v "$memory"
ulimit -s "$stack_size"
./festival

Default Linux stacks are small (8 MB), which kills deep recursion — a plain dfs(200000) crashes with a stack overflow on a default machine but runs happily under the IOI's 2 GB stack. The IOI does this on purpose: recursion depth is not a trap they want in the game. When you practice at home, run your binary the same way (ulimit -s 2097152; ./festival works on your Mac too), and you will never discover a stack difference between home and the contest.

7.5The rules that come with the format
Protocol Violation — the modern way to score zero

The grading rules state it plainly: submissions must not perform explicit input or output operations — no cin/cout, no scanf/printf, no file access, and no exit() calls. All data flows through the task's interface functions. A program that violates this receives the Protocol Violation verdict and scores nothing for that subtask. The single allowed exception is standard error: your cerr debug lines are legal and invisible to the grader. Before every submission, scan your file once for stray cout — it is a 5-second check that has saved whole contests.

A closing word on the other two task styles so nothing on contest day can surprise you. Communication tasks (like Souvenirs, or Migrations from the same year) give you extra grader functions to call inside your implementation, with a call budget printed in the statement; your Part 6 instincts still work — the sample grader still reads an input file and prints a report — but now debugging also means counting your own calls with a cerr counter. Output-only subtasks hand you the input files up front and ask you to submit .out files you produced by any method — brute force, heuristics, even by hand for tiny cases; CMS simply has an "output file" upload slot instead of a source slot. Both styles plug into exactly the workflow you already own.

Part 08 — Automation
judge.sh: Build Your Own Grader

You now own every ingredient of testing by hand. Time to make the machine do the typing. A judge script is a small text file of shell commands that runs your solution on every test in a folder, compares outputs, colors the verdict, times every run, and prints a summary — your personal Codeforces, living in your task folder, no internet required. Contestants who build this habit test ten times more often than contestants who type the loop by hand, and testing ten times more often is, mechanically, how you find more bugs than your rivals.

8.1Scripts 101 — the thirty-second version

A shell script is just commands saved in a file, executed top to bottom, exactly as if you typed them. Make your first one now. The first line — the shebang — tells the system which shell language the file is written in; always use bash for scripts (the IOI machines' native shell), even though your interactive Mac prompt is zsh:

hello.sh — your first script
$ cd ~/ioi/2025/festival
$ echo 'echo "scripts work!"' > hello.sh
$ bash hello.sh          # run it with bash explicitly
scripts work!
$ chmod +x hello.sh       # one-time: mark it executable
$ ./hello.sh               # now it runs directly, like a command
scripts work!

That is the whole ceremony: write commands in a file, run it with bash file.sh, or mark it executable once with chmod +x and run it with ./file.sh forever after. The permission denied error you may have already met simply means you skipped the chmod step — Appendix B lists it, but now you know it cold.

8.2The minimal judge — five lines to memorize

Before the deluxe version, burn this one into memory, because you can retype it from scratch at the contest in twenty seconds. It is nothing more than the Part 6 loop wearing a file:

minijudge.sh — the loop, weaponized
#!/bin/bash
for f in tests/*.in; do
  ./sol < "$f" | diff - "${f%.in}.out" > /dev/null && echo "PASS  $f" || echo "FAIL  $f"
done

Save it as minijudge.sh next to your compiled sol (or festival — same idea), run bash minijudge.sh, and watch a wall of verdicts. This five-liner already covers the classic stdin/stdout tasks and the modern grader tasks alike, because both are just "run binary, feed file, compare."

8.3The full judge — colors, timing, summary

Now the version you will actually use daily. It accepts the solution and test folder as arguments, handles both .out and .ans extensions, times each run with /usr/bin/time, flags runs that flirt with the time limit, and prints a final tally. Create it once, copy it into every task folder, and edit only the LIMIT line per task:

judge.sh — your personal grader
#!/bin/bash
# judge.sh — run every test in a folder against a solution binary.
# Usage:  bash judge.sh <binary> <test-folder>     e.g.  bash judge.sh festival examples

SOL=${1:-sol}          # 1st argument: solution binary (default "sol")
DIR=${2:-tests}        # 2nd argument: test folder  (default "tests")
LIMIT=1.0             # time limit in seconds — EDIT PER TASK (statement!)
GREEN='\033[32m'; RED='\033[31m'; YELL='\033[33m'; OFF='\033[0m'

pass=0; fail=0
for IN in "$DIR"/*.in; do
  name=$(basename "$IN" .in)
  ans="$DIR/$name.out"
  [ -f "$ans" ] || ans="$DIR/$name.ans"          # accept either extension

  /usr/bin/time -p "$SOL" < "$IN" > /tmp/j.out 2> /tmp/j.time
  sec=$(grep '^real' /tmp/j.time | awk '{print $2}')

  if diff -q /tmp/j.out "$ans" > /dev/null; then
    printf "  ${GREEN}PASS${OFF}  %-12s  %ss\n" "$name" "$sec"
    pass=$((pass+1))
  else
    printf "  ${RED}FAIL${OFF}  %-12s  %ss\n" "$name" "$sec"
    fail=$((fail+1))
  fi

  slow=$(awk -v t="$sec" -v l="$LIMIT" 'BEGIN{print (t>=l)?1:0}')
  [ "$slow" = "1" ] && printf "        ${YELL}over ${LIMIT}s — too slow for the server${OFF}\n"
done

echo "  --------------------------------"
echo "  $pass passed, $fail failed"

Also note the timing measures your Mac, not the grading server — treat the numbers as a guide, with the half-the-limit rule from Part 5 as your safety margin.

Part 09 — The Medalist Skill
Stress Testing: Find Bugs Before CMS Does

Samples are the tip of the iceberg — Festival had 3 examples and 112 hidden tests. Between "passes all three examples" and "passes all 115" lives the entire difference between participants and medalists, and the professional way across that gap is stress testing: make the computer invent thousands of random tests, and check your fast solution against a slow, obviously-correct one on every single test. It requires no creativity, no intuition, no staring at code — just the three files you are about to build. This is the single highest-leverage technique in this entire manual.

9.1The three files of a stress setup
FileRole
sol.cppThe candidate. Your fast, clever, possibly buggy solution (compiled as sol)
brute.cppThe oracle. A slow, stupid, bulletproof solution — brute force over everything. Correctness must be obvious, speed irrelevant (compiled as brute)
gen.pyThe factory. A Python script that prints one random test, different every time it runs

The logic: any test where sol and brute disagree is, with near certainty, a test where sol is wrong (the brute force is too simple to be wrong). The computer generates, both programs answer, the shell compares, thousands of times per minute. When they disagree, the loop stops and hands you the exact input that breaks your solution — a bug you would otherwise have discovered as a red subtask on contest day.

9.2A complete worked example — with a real bug

Task: read n, then n integers, print the maximum. Here is a fast solution carrying the most classic bug in the sport — initializing the maximum to zero:

sol.cpp — fast, and secretly broken
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

int main() {
    int n; cin >> n;
    int best = 0;                          // the bug: fails when all values are negative
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) { int x; cin >> x; best = max(best, x); }
    cout << best << "\n";
}
brute.cpp — slow, stupid, correct
#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;

int main() {
    int n; cin >> n;
    vector<int> v(n);
    for (auto &x : v) cin >> x;
    sort(v.begin(), v.end());               // sorting cannot be wrong about the max
    cout << v.back() << "\n";
}

The generator is where the art lives. Rules of a good gen.py: keep tests small (so the brute force stays fast and boundary behavior shows clearly), make every parameter random but biased toward the edges (include minimum sizes, duplicates, negatives), and take a seed argument so any failing test can be regenerated on demand:

gen.py — random tests, biased toward bugs
#!/usr/bin/env python3
import random, sys

seed = int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 0
random.seed(seed)

n = random.randint(1, 6)                  # tiny — brute force territory
print(n)
print(*[random.randint(-10, 10) for _ in range(n)])  # negatives included on purpose

And the loop that runs the whole factory — generate, run both, compare, stop on the first disagreement, keeping the criminal evidence:

stress.sh — the infinite bug hunter
#!/bin/bash
for i in $(seq 1 10000); do
  python3 gen.py $i > stress.in
  ./brute < stress.in > stress.ans
  ./sol   < stress.in > stress.out
  if ! diff -q stress.out stress.ans > /dev/null; then
    echo "MISMATCH on seed $i — saved as stress.in"
    echo "--- input:";     cat stress.in
    echo "--- expected:"; cat stress.ans
    echo "--- mine:";     cat stress.out
    exit 0
  fi
done
echo "10000 tests, no differences found."
Terminal — the hunt succeeds in seconds
$ g++ -std=gnu++20 -O2 -o sol sol.cpp && g++ -std=gnu++20 -O2 -o brute brute.cpp
$ bash stress.sh
MISMATCH on seed 3 — saved as stress.in
--- input:
4
-9 -3 -7 -1
--- expected:
-1
--- mine:
0

Read what just happened: in under a second, the factory found the exact bug, handed you a three-line reproduction, and made the fix obvious — initialize best to the first element (or negative infinity) instead of zero. No staring, no guessing, no "works on my machine." This loop — fast solution, brute oracle, random factory — is how strong contestants spend the middle hours of every contest, and why their submissions score full subtasks on the first try.

9.3What to do the moment a mismatch appears
  • Save the seed. python3 gen.py 3 regenerates the exact failing test forever — paste the seed number into a comment in your code while you debug.
  • Reproduce by hand. ./sol < stress.in and ./brute < stress.in side by side; the difference between their outputs is the whole story.
  • X-ray it. Drop cerr traces into sol.cpp at the suspicious steps, or recompile with -fsanitize=address,undefined (Part 5) if you suspect memory sins.
  • Shrink it. Edit stress.in down to the smallest input that still fails — a two-element failure is understood ten times faster than a twenty-element one. Then fix, re-run stress.sh, and let it run to 10000 clean tests before you trust the fix.
9.4The edge-case checklist — bugs love these

Random generators find broad classes of bugs automatically, but some corners need deliberate aiming. Before submitting any solution, interrogate it against this list — each line is a real subtask someone lost points to:

Minimum size. n = 1, empty sequence, single node, no edges — off-by-one bugs live here.
Maximum size. The full constraint values — for overflow, for time limit, for memory.
All equal. Every element identical breaks algorithms that rely on distinctness.
All negative / all zeros. The exact bug our stress hunt just caught.
Ties and boundary values. Sums hitting exactly the constraint limit, answers of exactly 0 or exactly k.
Already sorted / reverse sorted. The classic quicksort and DP killers.
Impossible cases. If the answer can be "−1" or "IMPOSSIBLE", make sure both branches are reachable and printed exactly as specified.
9.5Stress testing grader tasks — the modern twist

For interface tasks (Part 7) the same trio works with one adaptation: brute.cpp implements the same interface slowly, and instead of compiling each solution alone, you compile each with the sample grader: g++ -std=gnu++20 -O2 -o sol grader.cpp festival.cpp and g++ -std=gnu++20 -O2 -o brute grader.cpp brute.cpp (keep the brute in a separate file that also includes festival.h). The stress loop is then byte-for-byte identical — generate, run both binaries, diff. For interactive tasks, stress testing the full protocol is harder, but you can still stress the algorithm by writing your own tiny simulator of the grader's rules; even that partial honesty catches most bugs.

The 80/20 of contest debugging

When CMS feedback says a subtask failed (Part 10), your first move is not rereading code — it is stress testing with constraints matching that subtask. Set gen.py's ranges to the subtask's limits, run the loop, and let the mismatch tell you what the hidden test contained. Debugging starts with reproduction; stress testing is reproduction on demand.

That countdown is your heartbeat for five hours — glance, never stare. Announcements appear here when the jury corrects or clarifies something for everyone; strong contestants re-check this page every time they switch tasks, because missing an announcement means solving yesterday's version of the problem.

10.2The task page and its downloads

Clicking a task opens its page, which holds four things: the statement (PDF — open it in the browser's viewer or download and open in any PDF reader), the attachment package (the zip from Part 7), the submission box, and your submission history for this task. Reading order that works: open the statement, read it fully once without writing code, then download the package and unzip it. The statement's subtask table tells you the point menu; the package's examples/ gives you the first verdicts. Both downloads are also linked from the practice competition, so you will have rehearsed this exact clicking before it counts.

10.3Submitting — the full ceremony
1
Open the task page, find the submission box
A file chooser and a language selector. Select your festival.cpp — the single source file, never the compiled binary, never the grader, never a zip. If the language selector exists, pick C++ (recent IOIs accept only C++ anyway).
2
Press submit and watch the statuses
The submission appears in your history and moves through states: compiling (the server runs the compiler — a compile error shows the message here, fix and resubmit), then evaluating (your program runs against every hidden test, one at a time), then the score table appears. Evaluation of a full submission typically takes a minute or two; use the wait to re-read your code or start the next thought.
3
Read the feedback like a report card
One row per subtask: points achieved out of the subtask's maximum. A fully passed subtask shows full marks. For a subtask that is not fully solved, CMS additionally reveals the number of the first failing test among its lowest-scoring tests, plus a one-line reason. You never see the test contents or your program's output — just enough to know what kind of failure happened where.
4
Iterate
Feedback says subtask 3 died with "Execution timed out" on test 3-07? Your complexity is too high for those constraints. "Output isn't correct" on 3-02? Aim gen.py at subtask 3's constraints and stress (Part 9). This feedback→hypothesis→fix→resubmit loop is the contest; everything else in this manual exists to make this loop fast.

10.5Questions — your hotline to the jury

Every CMS has a questions page. Use it when a statement is genuinely ambiguous — an unclear constraint, a contradictory example, an undefined term — and phrase it as a yes/no question quoting the page and line ("Page 2, Subtask 3: does 'adjacent' include the capital itself?"). The jury answers to you privately, and if the answer matters to everyone it becomes a public announcement. Never ask "is my approach right" or "what does this task mean" — those get declined, and the wait costs you minutes. Between questions, the page doubles as your announcement inbox, which is the real reason to visit it periodically.

10.6Submission strategy — spend them like a rich person

You have 50 submissions per task and one per minute; the per-minute gate lifts for the final 15 minutes. Strategy in four rules. First: submit something early — the brute force from hour one banks the small subtasks permanently (scores never decrease, Part 1). Second: submit at every meaningful milestone, not just at completion; a 60-point version on the server is worth more than a 100-point version that never leaves your machine. Third: before the last 15 minutes, ensure your best current version of every task is the one most recently submitted — the final score is the max per subtask, but only for things you actually uploaded. Fourth: keep a mental budget: a typical medal run uses 5–15 submissions per task; if you are past 30, you are debugging by resubmission, which is a smell — go back to stress testing.

Part 11 — Dress Rehearsal
Mock Contest Day: The Full Simulation

Knowledge becomes speed only through rehearsal under pressure. The first simulation feels awful for everyone; by the fourth, the timeline below will feel like a familiar road. That familiarity — not any single trick — is what you are actually building.

11.1The timeline of a winning day
ClockPhase and mission
0:00 – 0:20Setup and reconnaissance. Login, verify CMS, download all three packages and statements. Read all three statements once. Rank tasks by (points you expect) ÷ (time you expect). Panic is forbidden; this phase is reading, not solving.
0:20 – 0:50First blood. Take the easiest task. Type the brute force immediately, even before thinking hard — it becomes your oracle for stress and often earns subtask points by itself. Submit it if it compiles and passes examples.
0:50 – 2:00Deep work, task one. Solve the easy task properly: algorithm, implement, judge.sh on all examples plus hand tests, stress if a brute exists, submit. Leave only when the remaining gap needs an idea you do not have.
2:00 – 3:30The middle game. Move to task two. Same loop. If stuck for 30 consecutive minutes on one idea, switch tasks or switch subtasks — the 30-minute rule exists because sunk cost is the deadliest contest sin.
3:30 – 4:30Harvest. Every task gets a current-best submission on the server. Return to whatever has the cheapest remaining points: an unfinished subtask 4 here, a special case there. Partial credit compounds.
4:30 – 4:45Stabilize. No new algorithms. Fix known bugs, re-run judge.sh everywhere, re-submit best versions. Drink water, stretch your hands, breathe.
4:45 – 5:00The last 15. Submission rate limit is lifted. Final versions out. Never let unsubmitted code exist at 4:50 — the clock does not negotiate.
11.2The three iron rules of contest time
  • The 30-minute rule. Any single idea that has consumed 30 minutes without progress gets abandoned — switch task, switch subtask, or switch approach. The contest rewards opportunity-hopping, not heroism.
  • The always-submitted rule. At every moment of the contest, the server holds your best current solution for every task. Crashes, blackouts, and panics then cost you nothing. This rule alone has saved medal totals.
  • The brute-first rule. Before any clever algorithm, write the stupid one. It is your stress oracle, your partial credit, and often your understanding of the problem — the clever version arrives faster after the brute exists.
11.3Before the clock starts — the practice day and morning checklist

The day before contest day one is the practice competition: same CMS, same submissions, same grading. Its tasks are published in advance and you may bring printed solutions — so prepare them, and spend the session testing the pipeline, not the problems: log in, download a package, unzip, compile with ./compile, run the examples, submit, read feedback, ask one dummy question, and check the terminal's ulimit -s. Ten minutes of ceremony, rehearsed once, removes all of its power to surprise you tomorrow.

Slept 8 hours; ate breakfast; water bottle on desk.
Own wired keyboard/mouse brought (if using) and tested at the practice session.

11.4When things go wrong — the emergency protocols
  • Machine freezes or crashes. Raise your hand immediately — staff exist for exactly this. Your submissions live on the server, and your files survive reboots; the contest clock keeps running only in the sense that staff can grant time compensation, which they do. Never spend five minutes self-repairing what a volunteer fixes in one.
  • Your code file vanishes (accidental overwrite, wrong folder). CMS kept every version you submitted — open your submission history and re-download your last festival.cpp. This is the quiet superpower of the always-submitted rule.
  • Panic arrives (it visits everyone). The protocol: hands off keyboard, eyes closed, three slow breaths, then type the brute force for whatever task is in front of you. Brute force is the panic-proof activity — it needs no inspiration, and it always pays something.
  • Announcement lands mid-solve. Finish the current line of thought, then read it fully and re-check any constraint it touches before continuing.

12.3ioi_judge.py — grade yourself on official data

Save this next to your unzipped task folder. It compiles your solution with the official grader, runs every test in its subtask groups, and prints the score table you would have seen on CMS. (Note it compares token-by-token rather than byte-by-byte, which politely ignores whitespace differences — the same forgiveness real graders extend.)

ioi_judge.py — your private IOI grader
#!/usr/bin/env python3
# Usage: python3 ioi_judge.py <task-folder> <your-solution.cpp>
#   e.g. python3 ioi_judge.py festival my_festival.cpp
import json, glob, os, subprocess, sys, time

task = sys.argv[1]
sol  = sys.argv[2]
name = os.path.splitext(os.path.basename(sol))[0]

meta = json.load(open(f"{task}/problem.json"))
limit = meta["time_limit"]
binary = f"{name}_official"

# compile with the OFFICIAL grader — it speaks the secret test format
subprocess.run(["g++", "-std=gnu++20", "-O2", "-o", binary,
               f"{task}/graders/grader.cpp", sol], check=True)

total = earned = 0
for sub in sorted(glob.glob(f"{task}/subtasks/*.json")):
    info = json.load(open(sub))
    ok = True; worst = 0.0
    for tc in info["testcases"]:
        tin  = open(f"{task}/tests/{tc}.in")
        t0 = time.time()
        r = subprocess.run([f"./{binary}"], stdin=tin,
                           capture_output=True, text=True)
        worst = max(worst, time.time() - t0)
        want = open(f"{task}/tests/{tc}.out").read().split()
        if r.stdout.split() != want: ok = False
    mark = "PASS" if ok else "FAIL"
    print(f"  {mark}  subtask {info['score']:>5.1f} pts   worst {worst:.2f}s / {limit}s")
    total += info["score"]; earned += info["score"] if ok else 0

print(f"  ------------------------------------------")
print(f"  YOU SCORED: {earned:.1f} / {total:.1f}")
